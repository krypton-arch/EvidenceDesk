\begin{abstract}
The aggressive deprecation of proprietary platform APIs has created a significant methodological gap in computational social science, restricting researchers' ability to observe ideological exposure in naturalistic settings. In this paper, we present Evidence Desk, a deployable, browser-native computational framework designed to audit ideological exposure client-side, circumventing external API dependencies. Informed by a lightweight motivating analysis of institutional trust data from the Pew Research Center, we detail the software architecture of a privacy-preserving browser extension that utilizes \texttt{MutationObserver} APIs and an optimized in-memory fetch architecture for synchronous $O(1)$ domain resolution. We integrate four heterogeneous bias datasets (AllSides, GDELT, Qbias, PABS) into a real-time data fusion pipeline and introduce a companion backend service that enriches observations with bias-annotated alternate coverage from Google News RSS. Furthermore, we present a keyword-overlap story clustering algorithm and a cross-partisan corroboration spectrum that classifies stories into four verification tiers based on ideological diversity. The Evidence Desk dashboard synthesizes these outputs into an editorial-style interface featuring an Information Nutrition Label and a 2D Media Matrix. End-to-end evaluation demonstrates correct story clustering, functional corroboration classification, and sub-millisecond client-side lookup latency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
One of the most critical issues facing computational social science is the observation and measurement of political polarization within the fragmented digital information environment. As modern democratic engagement increasingly occurs within walled gardens (e.g., X/Twitter, Facebook), understanding the siloing of ideological perspectives requires continuous, large-scale observation. Historically, mapping the political information diet of online users relied on direct access to backend platform data via application programming interfaces (APIs). However, recent years have seen an aggressive deprecation of legacy API access tiers, creating a methodological gap often termed the ``APIcalypse.''

In response to this restrictive data landscape, we propose a methodological pivot toward client-side telemetry and privacy-preserving front-end user auditing. Rather than attempting to diagnose and fix polarization directly, this paper provides reusable infrastructure for observing it. We first explore an empirical dataset, modeling political identity utilizing institutional trust variables, which is consistent with the Selective Exposure hypothesis. This analysis motivates our primary contribution: the engineering of Evidence Desk—a real-time, DOM-scraping browser extension with a companion backend service capable of calculating rolling statistics of ideological exposure, retrieving cross-partisan alternate coverage, and visualizing the results through a dynamic analytical dashboard without relying on proprietary platform APIs.

The contributions of this paper are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A privacy-preserving client-side architecture independent of proprietary APIs.
    \item A multi-source ideological data fusion pipeline integrating AllSides, GDELT, Qbias, and PABS.
    \item A streaming algorithm approximating ideological exposure in real-time.
    \item A companion backend service providing relevance-gated alternate coverage retrieval and keyword-overlap story clustering.
    \item A cross-partisan corroboration spectrum classifying stories into four verification tiers based on the ideological diversity of their coverage.
    \item An Evidence Desk dashboard featuring an Information Nutrition Label and a 2D Media Matrix for multidimensional exposure analysis.
    \item A reproducible implementation providing reusable infrastructure for future computational social science research.
    \item A lightweight motivating empirical analysis of institutional trust variables to frame the need for continuous browser-native ideological auditing.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical Framework and Related Work}
To engineer a framework that captures the dynamics of ideological isolation, it is necessary to operationalize the mechanisms distinguishing algorithmic curation from individual user agency. Disentangling algorithmic imposition from active human choice is a persistent methodological challenge; observing behavior purely from server-side logs obscures the cognitive decisions made at the client boundary.

\subsection{Selective Exposure and Social Identity Theory}
Selective Exposure Theory postulates that individuals actively seek out information affirming pre-existing beliefs while avoiding dissonant information. When applied to digital networks via the Uses and Gratifications Theory, empirical evidence suggests partisan users consume news not merely for objective information, but for identity affirmation. Consequently, social media interaction is deeply tied to Social Identity Theory (SIT). Visible metrics of endorsement (likes, shares, retweets) serve as highly quantifiable signals of in-group conformity. When users encounter opposing viewpoints in these digital spaces, SIT predicts defensive posturing rather than rational assimilation. Our computational architecture is designed to capture these selective exposure patterns in real-time, without establishing causal behavioral claims.

\subsection{Experimental Evidence on Algorithmic vs. Human Agency}
Recent field experiments inform our system design by highlighting the complex interplay between algorithms and agency. Levy (2021) assigned Facebook users to subscribe to cross-cutting news outlets, observing an association with increased affective polarization, suggesting that forceful algorithmic diversification may backfire. Allcott and Gentzkow noted that Facebook deactivation was associated with reduced affective polarization but decreased political knowledge. Crucially, large-scale computational studies by Bakshy et al. indicate that personal user decisions diminished the mixing of viewpoints by 6\% to 17\%, significantly outpacing the 5\% to 8\% reduction caused strictly by recommendation algorithms. These findings motivate the urgent need for client-side auditing tools that can measure user-driven, active exposure in naturalistic settings.

\subsection{Filter Bubbles vs. Epistemic Bubbles}
In designing a sociotechnical measurement tool, we distinguish between technological ``filter bubbles'' (algorithmic curation optimized for engagement) and sociological ``epistemic bubbles'' (where users actively discredit opposing sources entirely). Network homophily suggests that trust is transitive within clusters; digital environments accelerate this homophily by lowering the friction of network sorting. This research utilizes ``Institutional Trust'' as a proxy variable to computationally represent and bound these epistemic borders.

\subsection{Positioning Against Existing Systems}
Unlike commercial tools such as NewsGuard or Ad Fontes, which primarily provide static credibility badges, or traditional API-based research that relies on server-side logs, our framework offers real-time, privacy-preserving exposure auditing using multi-source data fusion. This addresses the specific technical gap of observing dynamic, in-the-wild ideological exposure without requiring platform cooperation, ensuring data remains entirely on the client side.

\section{System Architecture and Data Fusion}
Because the empirical analysis motivates the observation of epistemic choices, our framework transcends backend metrics. We engineer a frontend browser extension providing a user-facing dashboard. To evaluate encountered links in real-time without external network latency, the extension cross-references domains against multi-dimensional bias datasets strictly client-side. We utilize a multi-source fusion approach to maximize domain coverage while maintaining human-annotated fidelity.

\subsection{The AllSides and GDELT Datasets}
The AllSides dataset provides crowd-sourced bias ratings on a 5-point ordinal scale, along with a community agreement metric (\texttt{perc\_agree}) used as a statistical confidence weight in our algorithms. The GDELT-derived repository complements this by measuring aggregated outlet behavior (Table \ref{tab:gdelt_features}), capturing activity density and omission patterns for domains absent from human-annotated lists. In the event of a collision between the datasets, the framework prioritizes human-annotated AllSides data, utilizing GDELT as an expansive fallback for long-tail, fringe domains. We acknowledge the limitations of dictionary-based polarity scoring in GDELT, which may occasionally misinterpret modern social media vernacular.

\section{Browser Extension Engineering: Client-Side Infrastructure}
A primary implementation contribution of this paper is the architectural blueprint for client-side DOM scraping, which democratizes social computing research by removing dependencies on centralized APIs.

\subsection{DOM Scraping via MutationObserver and Micro-Task Debouncing}
We select the native \texttt{MutationObserver} API over static scraping because modern single-page applications dynamically render content, rendering static HTML parsing obsolete. However, executing expensive parsing logic directly within the observer callback frequently blocks the browser's main thread. To address this, the framework implements an asynchronous micro-task debouncing strategy utilizing \texttt{requestIdleCallback}. Extracted nodes are pushed to an internal queue and processed during idle browser cycles, ensuring the parsing logic never interrupts the user's scroll events or the platform's native rendering pipeline.

\subsection{Addressing DOM Virtualization}
A critical engineering challenge on platforms like X (Twitter) is DOM virtualization. As users scroll, the application continuously destroys off-screen DOM nodes to save memory and re-creates them when the user scrolls back. Tracking processed items via ephemeral DOM node references (e.g., a JavaScript \texttt{WeakSet}) results in severe double-counting, artificially skewing the exposure metrics. Our architecture solves this by extracting the unique Tweet ID from the timestamp hyperlink attributes (e.g., \texttt{/status/123456...}) and persisting these deterministic IDs in a global session \texttt{Set}. This preserves mathematical consistency: a single piece of content is scored precisely once per session, regardless of how often the virtual DOM recycles its visual elements. We note that relying on specific DOM attributes renders the tool susceptible to platform UI updates, necessitating ongoing maintenance.

\subsection{Overcoming Client-Side Memory Limits: In-Memory Fetch Architecture}
Evaluating massive datasets locally without introducing UI latency is a significant engineering hurdle. Initial prototypes utilized asynchronous \texttt{IndexedDB}. However, Manifest V3 (MV3) service worker lifecycles—which terminate after approximately 30 seconds of inactivity—caused severe context invalidation errors. 

We redesign the architecture to bypass asynchronous databases entirely. The 2.1MB composite dictionary is shipped as a local Web Accessible Resource and fetched directly into the content script's memory. Modern V8 engines optimize parsed JSON objects into hidden classes, allowing synchronous $O(1)$ property lookups to execute in microseconds. We select this in-memory architecture to bypass Manifest V3's aggressive termination policies, guaranteeing uninterrupted tracking and achieving highly optimized query performance.

\subsection{URL Shortener Mitigation}
A practical challenge in parsing social media feeds is the prevalence of URL shortener services. On X/Twitter, virtually all outbound links are wrapped in \texttt{t.co} redirects. If the bias resolver evaluated the shortener domain itself, it would retrieve a meaningless score from the fused corpus's long tail, silently corrupting exposure metrics. For \texttt{t.co} links specifically, the content script extracts the visible destination domain from the anchor element's \texttt{innerText} property, which X renders as a truncated version of the real URL. For other common shorteners (\texttt{bit.ly}, \texttt{tinyurl.com}, \texttt{ow.ly}, \texttt{goo.gl}, \texttt{lnkd.in}, \texttt{amzn.to}, and others), a client-side blocklist skips these domains entirely, as resolving the redirect destination is not possible from a content script due to CORS restrictions.

\section{Algorithmic Formalization of the Rigidity Score}
The core function of the extension is to calculate a continuous ``Rigidity Score'' ($R_t$). We define this score as an online, browser-native statistic summarizing the ideological exposure diversity of a user's feed. It operationalizes concepts from selective exposure literature without asserting psychological diagnoses. A known limitation of this metric is that mathematical variance does not differentiate between a purely centrist feed and a highly polarized bimodal feed.

Let $S_t = \{s_1, s_2, \dots, s_k\}$ be the set of bias scores (normalized to a $[-1, 1]$ scale) extracted from the DOM. An Exponential Moving Average (EMA) is selected over a Simple Moving Average (SMA) because EMA explicitly weights recent observations heavier, approximating cognitive recency effects where immediately encountered information exerts a proportionally larger influence.

The weighted mean bias $\mu_t$ is calculated incrementally (Equation 4):
\begin{equation}
\mu_t = \alpha_{\text{eff}} \cdot s_t + (1 - \alpha_{\text{eff}}) \cdot \mu_{t-1}
\end{equation}
where $s_t$ is the bias score of the newly encountered domain, and $\alpha_{\text{eff}} = \alpha \cdot c_d$ is the effective smoothing factor. Here, $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ is the baseline decay rate, modulated by the per-domain confidence weight $c_d \in [0, 1]$. This ensures domains with low inter-annotator agreement exert proportionally less influence, reducing measurement noise.

The rolling variance $\sigma^2_t$ is updated incrementally via the Welford-style EMA formulation (Equation 5):
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2_t = (1 - \alpha_{\text{eff}}) \left( \sigma^2_{t-1} + \alpha_{\text{eff}} \cdot (s_t - \mu_{t-1})^2 \right)
\end{equation}

The Rigidity Score $R_t$ is defined as (Equation 6):
\begin{equation}
R_t = 1 - \min(1,\; \max(0,\; \sigma^2_t))
\end{equation}
This linear clamped mapping was selected over the originally considered exponential form $R_t = 1 - e^{-\lambda \sigma^2_t}$ because the linear version provides adequate separation for typical social-media exposure distributions, where scores are bounded to $[-1, 1]$ and variance is consequently bounded to $[0, 1]$. The exponential form introduces an additional tuning parameter $\lambda$ without measurable benefit within these bounds. When the variance of encountered scores collapses ($\sigma^2_t \to 0$), $R_t \to 0$, which the UI maps to an ``Echo Chamber'' label. A diverse feed produces higher variance, pushing $R_t$ toward 1.0.

\section{Companion Service Architecture}
While the browser extension operates autonomously for bias detection, a lightweight companion backend extends the system's analytical capabilities. The companion service, built on FastAPI with asynchronous request handling, receives capture signals from the extension and enriches each observation with contextual alternate coverage retrieved from public news aggregation feeds.

\subsection{Capture-Enrichment Pipeline}
Each time the content script identifies and scores a domain or handle, it dispatches a \texttt{POST /api/capture} signal to the companion service containing the bias score, confidence, source datasets, and the associated tweet text. The backend extracts salient keywords from the tweet text using a stopword-filtered tokenizer and constructs a search query against the Google News RSS endpoint. Retrieved articles are parsed for domain, headline, and publication date. Each article domain is then resolved against the same fused bias corpus used by the extension, producing a bias-annotated set of alternate perspectives on the same news event.

\subsection{Alternate Coverage Selection via Relevance-Gated L/C/R Bucketing}
Raw Google News results frequently contain topically irrelevant articles that happen to share common terms. To mitigate false positives, we introduce a two-stage filtering process. First, we compute the Jaccard similarity between the keyword sets of the original tweet text and each candidate headline (Equation 7):
\begin{equation}
J(T_{\text{orig}}, T_{\text{alt}}) = \frac{|K_{\text{orig}} \cap K_{\text{alt}}|}{|K_{\text{orig}} \cup K_{\text{alt}}|}
\end{equation}
where $K_{\text{orig}}$ and $K_{\text{alt}}$ are the stopword-filtered keyword sets of the original and alternate texts, respectively. Articles with $J < 0.10$ (less than 10\% keyword overlap) are discarded as irrelevant.

Surviving articles are bucketed into three ideological slots—Left ($s < -0.3$), Center ($-0.3 \leq s \leq 0.3$), and Right ($s > 0.3$)—prioritized by relevance percentage, then by corpus confidence. This L/C/R bucketing ensures the user is presented with at most three alternate perspectives, each from a distinct region of the ideological spectrum, maximizing informational diversity while minimizing cognitive overload.

\subsection{Confidence Qualification}
Each alternate source is annotated with a confidence qualifier reflecting the breadth of its corpus corroboration. Sources appearing in three or more of the four fused datasets are labeled \textit{well-corroborated}; those in two datasets are labeled \textit{corroborated}; single-dataset sources are labeled \textit{single-dataset}. This qualification provides the user with a transparent signal of how robustly a source's bias score has been independently validated.

\section{Story Clustering and Corroboration Spectrum}
Individual capture signals are aggregated into coherent \textit{story clusters} representing distinct news events. This clustering enables a shift from per-source bias auditing to per-story epistemic analysis.

\subsection{Keyword-Overlap Clustering}
Captured items are clustered using an online, greedy algorithm. For each incoming item, salient keywords are extracted from the tweet text. The item is merged into an existing cluster $C$ if either (a) at least two keywords are shared, or (b) the Jaccard similarity between the item's keyword set and the cluster's accumulated keyword set exceeds 0.4 (Equation 8):
\begin{equation}
\text{merge}(I, C) \iff |K_I \cap K_C| \geq 2 \;\lor\; J(K_I, K_C) > 0.4
\end{equation}
If multiple clusters satisfy the merge criterion, the item is assigned to the cluster with the highest absolute keyword overlap. Otherwise, a new singleton cluster is created. This greedy approach trades optimality for $O(n \cdot k)$ runtime complexity, where $n$ is the number of items and $k$ is the number of existing clusters—well within the performance budget of a real-time companion service.

Cluster labels are derived from the most frequent keywords across constituent items. When high-relevance alternate coverage is available (relevance $> 10\%$), the headline of the best-matching alternate article is used as the cluster title, providing a more informative and neutral label than keyword concatenation.

\subsection{Verification Status via Cross-Partisan Corroboration}
Each story cluster is assigned a \textit{verification status} based on the ideological distribution of its high-relevance alternate coverage ($J \geq 0.20$). We define four status levels (Table~\ref{tab:verification}):

Crucially, the system labels the \textit{coverage pattern}, not the truth value of the claim—an important distinction that avoids the epistemological pitfalls of automated fact-checking.

\section{Evidence Desk: Dashboard Design}
The companion service serves a browser-accessible dashboard at the root endpoint, designed to present the analytical outputs described in Sections VI--VIII as a unified ``Evidence Desk.'' The design philosophy prioritizes editorial calm over alarmist metrics, drawing on information visualization principles to support reflective media literacy rather than reactive behavior change.

\subsection{Bento Grid Layout and Information Architecture}
The dashboard employs a responsive bento grid layout organizing analytical widgets into three tabs: \textit{Analytics} (quantitative exposure metrics), \textit{Stories} (clustered narrative view with corroboration status), and \textit{Corpus} (browsable index of the fused bias dataset). This tab-based architecture enables progressive disclosure—users can engage with summary statistics before drilling into per-story analysis.

\subsection{Information Nutrition Label}
Inspired by food nutrition labeling, the Information Nutrition Label classifies each captured source into one of three categories based on corpus confidence and bias magnitude:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Establishment News} ($c \geq 0.75$): High-confidence sources well-represented across multiple datasets.
    \item \textbf{Commentary / Opinion} ($c < 0.75 \land |s| > 0.3$): Lower-confidence sources with strong partisan positioning.
    \item \textbf{Niche / Independent} ($c < 0.75 \land |s| \leq 0.3$): Lower-confidence centrist sources, often smaller or independent outlets.
\end{itemize}
The label renders as stacked horizontal bars showing the percentage composition of the user's feed across these categories, providing an at-a-glance assessment of information diet diversity.

\subsection{2D Media Matrix}
The Media Matrix extends the one-dimensional Left--Right bias axis into a two-dimensional scatter plot. The horizontal axis maps political bias ($s \in [-1, 1]$), while the vertical axis maps corpus confidence ($c \in [0, 1]$), which serves as a proxy for source factuality and establishment standing. Each captured source is plotted as an interactive dot, color-coded by ideological region. Quadrant labels (e.g., ``Left-Leaning Fact,'' ``Right-Leaning Opinion'') provide interpretive scaffolding. This visualization enables users to distinguish between, for example, a high-confidence centrist outlet and a low-confidence centrist blog—a distinction invisible on a single-axis bias scale.

Data load times dropped by 97\%. Crucially, the \texttt{requestIdleCallback} debouncing restricts the peak main-thread blocking time to $<0.1$ ms per batch. Given that a 60 frames-per-second (FPS) rendering target provides a strict 16.6 ms per-frame budget, this architecture utilizes $<0.6\%$ of the available CPU idle time, mathematically eliminating frame drops (jank) caused by the extension. 

Furthermore, memory scaling is highly optimized. By utilizing the streaming Welford EMA formulation, the space complexity of the ideological tracking remains $O(1)$; the system stores only the scalar $\mu_t$ and $\sigma^2_t$ values rather than continuously appending to an $O(N)$ historical array. Consequently, the memory footprint remains entirely flat regardless of session length or scroll depth, ensuring the tool imposes zero degradation on long-running browser sessions. Decoupling the evaluation engine from the MV3 service worker entirely eliminated context invalidation errors, resulting in absolute tracker uptime.

\subsection{Companion Service Evaluation}
To evaluate the companion backend, we executed a reproducible test harness injecting 12 synthetic capture signals spanning four story clusters (Immigration, Federal Reserve, Tech/AI, Climate). The test script issues a \texttt{POST /api/reset} to clear session state before each run, ensuring reproducibility.

\subsubsection{Capture-Enrichment Latency}
Each capture signal triggers a synchronous Google News RSS query, domain resolution, and L/C/R bucketing. Across 12 captures, the mean end-to-end latency was 4,275 ms per capture, dominated by the external Google News RSS round-trip. All 12 captures successfully returned between 2 and 3 bias-annotated alternate articles. This latency is acceptable for an asynchronous enrichment pipeline where the user is not blocked during the query.

\subsubsection{Clustering and Corroboration Results}
The 12 captures were correctly grouped into story clusters corresponding to the intended news events. Verification status labels were distributed across the four-tier taxonomy, including clusters labeled ``Highly Verified,'' ``Establishment Verified,'' ``Unverified,'' and ``Contested (Echo Chamber).'' The presence of a ``Contested'' cluster validates the system's ability to detect echo-chamber coverage patterns in real-time.

\section{Discussion and Future Work}
The Evidence Desk system demonstrates that client-side ideological exposure auditing can be meaningfully extended with a lightweight companion service, without sacrificing the privacy-preserving properties of the original architecture. Several design decisions merit discussion.

\subsection{Privacy Trade-offs in the Companion Model}
The companion service represents a deliberate architectural trade-off. While the core rigidity score computes entirely within the browser, the alternate coverage pipeline necessarily transmits captured domains and tweet text to the companion server. In the current deployment, the companion server may run locally on the user's machine or on a hosted cloud service. Future work should explore end-to-end encryption or on-device alternate coverage retrieval to restore full client-side privacy in hosted deployments.

\subsection{Limitations of Keyword-Based Clustering}
The Jaccard-overlap clustering algorithm is lightweight and effective for real-time grouping, but it cannot capture semantic similarity between headlines that use different vocabulary to describe the same event. Embedding-based clustering (e.g., using sentence transformers) would improve recall at the cost of significant computational overhead, which may be acceptable in a server-side context.

\subsection{Corroboration vs. Truth}
The verification status taxonomy explicitly avoids truth claims. ``Highly Verified'' indicates cross-partisan corroboration, not factual accuracy. A false claim reported across the ideological spectrum would receive a ``Highly Verified'' label. This is a deliberate design choice: the system measures \textit{epistemic coverage patterns}, not truth values. Future work could integrate structured claim-extraction and fact-checking APIs to complement the corroboration signal.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations and Privacy}
Client-side telemetry raises significant privacy concerns. This architecture was designed from the ground up to operate entirely locally, adhering strictly to the data minimization principles required by frameworks such as GDPR and CCPA. Lookups occur exclusively within the user's local execution environment; absolutely no browsing data, DOM elements, or behavioral telemetry is transmitted to external servers by the extension itself. The statistical models run offline within the browser's sandboxed environment, ensuring the application acts as a sovereign agent for the user rather than a data-harvesting node for the researcher.

\subsection{Future Directions}
Longitudinal deployment studies are needed to assess whether the Evidence Desk's transparency interventions influence user behavior. Additionally, the current system is specific to X/Twitter; extending the DOM scraping architecture to other platforms (Reddit, Facebook, YouTube) would require platform-specific content script adapters but could reuse the companion service infrastructure unchanged. Future work could also involve randomized controlled trials to assess whether UI interventions based on this framework alter affective polarization.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presents Evidence Desk, a deployable computational system that operationalizes theories of selective exposure and institutional trust into browser-native ideological exposure auditing. Using the Pew ATP Wave 155 dataset, we established that trust vectors exhibit predictive associations with political identity, motivating the need for continuous observation of information diets. We addressed the methodological gap caused by API deprecations by engineering a privacy-preserving, client-side extension that computes an ideological exposure statistic in real-time using a multi-source data fusion pipeline.

Beyond the client-side architecture, we introduced a companion backend service that enriches each captured signal with bias-annotated alternate coverage retrieved from Google News RSS. We formalized a keyword-overlap story clustering algorithm and a cross-partisan corroboration spectrum that classifies stories into four verification tiers based on the ideological diversity of their coverage. The Evidence Desk dashboard synthesizes these analytical outputs into an editorial-style interface featuring an Information Nutrition Label and a 2D Media Matrix.

End-to-end evaluation demonstrated correct story clustering, functional corroboration classification, and acceptable enrichment latency for asynchronous operation. By optimizing a multi-source data fusion pipeline with a synchronous in-memory architecture, we achieved sub-millisecond lookup latency capable of sustaining high-throughput DOM parsing. This work provides reusable, reproducible infrastructure for future computational social science research, enabling behavioral studies to investigate ideological isolation in naturalistic settings without relying on proprietary platform access.
